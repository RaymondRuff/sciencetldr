Nadia: And today, in Cell Chemical Biology, Qiu and colleagues at Sun Yat-sen University: "Shared alpha-helix chimeraffibodies enable linker-free bispecific protein design." The short version — if you want one protein to grab two targets, you normally build two binding domains and tie them together with a floppy linker. These authors dropped the linker and made the two domains share a helix.

Theo: Okay. So why is that worth ten minutes? Bispecifics aren't a new idea.

Nadia: Because of the method, not the molecule. The linker is flexible, so you get conformational heterogeneity; exposed, so proteases find it; and non-human, which is a place immune recognition can start. The usual answer is a better linker; this is a geometric one.

Theo: By overlapping the two domains.

Nadia: Right. Both binders are alpha-helical bundles. So: can you find a stretch of helix where the two domains superimpose closely enough that one physical helix does both jobs?

Theo: Set the pieces up. What are the two binders?

Nadia: The scaffold is an affibody — a small bundle of alpha helices. One is Z three forty-two, which binds H-E-R-two. The others bind human serum albumin: A-B-D zero thirty-five, and one called G-A, a module from protein G.

Theo: And albumin is there for half-life.

Nadia: Exactly. A binder this small clears fast through the kidney. Bind albumin and the complex sits above the glomerular filtration threshold, so you ride along with it.

Theo: And overlapping two helical bundles deforms both binding surfaces at once.

Nadia: That's the whole problem. The binding sites sit on those helices.

Theo: So how do they search for an overlap that works?

Nadia: A pipeline they call AlphaSync. It takes contiguous backbone fragments — four to fourteen residues — from each domain, superimposes every compatible pair, and scores on backbone alignment, clashes and length. Then Protein M-P-N-N redesign on the survivors, with AlphaFold three and Rosetta energies to triage.

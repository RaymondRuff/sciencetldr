**Nadia:** Hello and welcome to Science TLDR. I'm Nadia.

**Theo:** And I'm Theo.

**Nadia:** Today, a paper called "It's Like You're Trying to Defuse a Bomb" — fear, responsibility and shame in young men's sexual lives, in the Journal of Sex and Marital Therapy. Thirty-one interviews with heterosexual men aged eighteen to thirty-two, thematically analysed. What the authors lead with is how rarely these men described enjoying sex.

**Theo:** Thirty-one interviews. Qualitative, one sitting each?

**Nadia:** One sitting. Sixty-four to a hundred and twenty-four minutes, mean of ninety-two, all by the first author, in person or by videoconference, late 2023.

**Theo:** Okay. Make the case. Why this one?

**Nadia:** Two reasons. One, this interior account is thin in the literature — the authors note even the qualitative work on men who have perpetrated sexual violence is narrow, dated and drawn mainly from incarcerated samples. Two, and more interesting: what they report is not what they went looking for. The guide has seven questions, and none asks how sex feels.

**Theo:** Wait — then where does the emotional material come from?

**Nadia:** Asides. Mid-anecdote. The first author noticed during fieldwork that participants kept returning to what they felt, what they held themselves responsible for, what they feared — then went back and coded all thirty-one transcripts line by line. A legitimate inductive move, and also the paper's central vulnerability. Hold that thought.

**Theo:** Noted. Set the scene — what's this "age of consent" framing?

**Nadia:** It's from Srinivasan, 2021. Over the last decade, consent has become the organising concept in how young people are taught about sex — school programmes, campaigns, law reform. And the emphasis falls on young men: ask, listen, make sure. That's the feminist legal shift: from whether she consented to whether he took care to find out.

**Theo:** Right. And these men grew up entirely inside that.

**Nadia:** Entirely. The same team has an earlier paper on this cohort, on how they decide someone is consenting. All of them could state a conventional definition; what they couldn't describe was applying it. Instead they stacked up signals — mutuality, trust, timing, the occasional check-in — until they felt sure enough. They call it multi-factor authentication.

**Theo:** And this paper asks how that stacking feels.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Three themes. First, anxiety and vigilance. Twenty-nine of thirty-one described fear, nervousness or pressure around sex — ninety-four percent of the cohort. The same twenty-nine described it shaping behaviour: constant monitoring of a partner's signals, and of themselves.

**Theo:** Give me the texture.

**Nadia:** The title quote is from a participant they call Ernest, twenty-eight. "It's like you're trying to defuse a bomb, but you don't know which wire to cut." Then — "it's a very tense situation in one that's like, meant to be enjoyable and open and free." An eighteen-year-old says he's "gotten so used to" the fear that it "feels natural"; a nineteen-year-old, "every time I did it, I did it with fear."

**Theo:** Hmm. How much of that is just being young and anxious about sex? Performance anxiety has been around a while.

**Nadia:** Right challenge, and they don't dodge it. Performance anxiety is in there explicitly — satisfying a partner, lasting long enough, being judged. One man says both in a breath: "you are going to be judged for not being able to, and the frustration of not being able to either." Their read is that for these men the two were hard to separate. But — well, they can't separate them either.

**Theo:** Fair. Theme two?

**Nadia:** Fear of causing harm. Also twenty-nine of thirty-one, substantially overlapping with the first group. It's about wanting to be good. One participant: "I don't want to scare someone or make them be creeped out... even though I don't have any bad intentions." The authors argue that concern tips over into people-pleasing and what they call self-erasure.

**Theo:** Meaning what, concretely?

**Nadia:** Men describing sex they weren't enthusiastic about, performed for the partner's benefit. Ernest, asked by a partner what he liked, said "I don't know" — and it shocked him. One twenty-one-year-old's strategy was handing the encounter over: "I'll simply just let them control, like full control." The authors note that handing over control could protect him as much as please a partner.

**Theo:** Huh. Sharper than I expected.

**Nadia:** It recurs. Theme three is shame, and they split it. Twenty-six of thirty-one — eighty-four percent — described sex as high stakes. Within that, nineteen, sixty-one percent, feared formal consequences: accusation, legal trouble, career. Seventeen, fifty-five percent, feared the informal version — gossip, reputation, exclusion. And sixteen, fifty-two percent, turned that same logic inward.

**Theo:** The inward one is the actual claim, isn't it.

**Nadia:** It's the thesis. Men describing themselves as "not a good person," judging themselves hard. The authors' phrasing: the question becomes not only does she want this, but am I the kind of man who could get this wrong. Ernest again — young men feel like "ticking time bombs for sexual assault." In their reading, cultural suspicion has been internalised: these men see themselves as the bomb.

**Theo:** And the exclusion piece — real cases or imagined ones?

**Nadia:** Both, mixed, and the paper can't separate them. One man recounts an acquaintance who made lewd advances and was, in his word, excommunicated from his music scene. A twenty-year-old critiques the norm — "the accepted model is to throw them out" — then says the judgment for staying friends is too strong. Two others describe cutting off a friend themselves.

**Theo:** Okay. The hard part. What I keep circling is the instrument. The guide never asked about feelings — but it did ask about something.

**Nadia:** Yeah. Questions three to five ask for stories where the participant didn't consent, or wasn't sure the partner consented, or where the partner didn't. Six asks why men go ahead when unsure. Four of seven point at uncertain and non-consensual encounters — and then the men talk about how sex feels.

**Theo:** That's not a small thing.

**Nadia:** It isn't, and to their credit the authors flag it themselves in the methods: these accounts should be read with that in mind. I'd have liked more than one paragraph on it. Because the headline claim is an absence — that they rarely described enjoying sex — and an absence is exactly what this instrument would manufacture.

**Theo:** Nobody asked what good sex was like.

**Nadia:** Nobody did — question two asks for a story where both were sure, but that's certainty, not pleasure. And a second rule interacts with this. They assessed all thirty-one transcripts per theme, but because participants weren't asked directly, non-mention wasn't treated as absence. Which is careful —

**Theo:** — but then what is ninety-four percent?

**Nadia:** It's ninety-four percent who spontaneously raised it. Not a prevalence estimate — the authors say so in as many words: these counts describe this cohort only. I suspect those percentages will travel further than that sentence does.

**Theo:** Say more about who the cohort is.

**Nadia:** Mostly one Australian university. Of the thirty-five men recruited, twenty-six came from campus flyers and nine by snowballing. Of the thirty-one here, twenty-four were in Australia, seven in the US; five interviews were in Spanish. Men charged or convicted of a sexual offence were ineligible. And these are volunteers for a study about consent, offered a forty-dollar grocery voucher.

**Theo:** So self-selected toward men who already think about this a lot.

**Nadia:** Almost certainly. Men who find consent unremarkable have little reason to sign up. The authors list the sampling limits plainly — one university, mostly Judeo-Christian upbringing, one interview each, no partners interviewed. But the discussion also suggests the pattern may reach young men across Western post-MeToo societies. Those don't sit easily together.

**Theo:** One more claim. They say these men overestimate the likelihood of a false accusation.

**Nadia:** They do, and they cite outside evidence — a Law Commission report, Victoria Police data — not anything in this dataset. The right call, I think. I'd just mark the boundary: the paper can document how the fear shaped these men's accounts. It can't measure the risk they feared. The authors say that too — their finding is about the fear, not the frequency.

**Theo:** And the quoting? One name keeps coming up.

**Nadia:** Ernest, twenty-eight, quoted more than anyone. There's a note in the methods saying so — the themes ran across the cohort, but he articulated plainly what younger men said more partially. An honest disclosure of a real risk: your impression of thirty-one men is set disproportionately by one articulate one.

**Theo:** This is the second qualitative interview paper recently where the counting is the pressure point.

**Nadia:** Episode sixty-eight, rejection sensitivity in ADHD — five participants, three themes, very vivid. Thirty-one is six times that and still small, but the move is identical: inductive coding of self-report, same risk that the themes are partly an artefact of what was asked.

**Theo:** And it rhymes with episode eighty-four, the white nationalism prevalence survey, where reverse-keying the question moved endorsement nine points. The number is downstream of the wording. Here there isn't even a wording to inspect, because the themes came from asides.

**Nadia:** Fair pairing. Though in eighty-four's favour, they could at least quantify the instrument effect. Here you can't.

**Theo:** Alright, three takeaways. One: in thirty-one self-selected young men, interviewed with a guide pointed at uncertain and non-consensual encounters, twenty-nine spontaneously described anxiety and vigilance, twenty-nine fear of causing harm, twenty-six shame. The overlap is the finding — the authors argue it's one experience, not three.

**Theo:** Two: the distinctive move is inward. Several men described themselves, rather than their partner's wishes, as the uncertain object. The authors tie that to shame versus guilt — guilt can prompt change, shame tends to prevent it. That's their interpretation, and it's testable.

**Theo:** Three: the percentages are not prevalence. They are counts of spontaneous mention in one cohort at one Australian university, and the authors say so. If you see a headline claiming ninety-four percent of young men fear sex, that is not this paper.

**Nadia:** What I'd watch for: a study that asks directly, with a guide that includes pleasure and good encounters, and ideally young women on the same schedule. If the inward turn is specific to these men, that design would show it.

**Theo:** Mine's simpler. Shame and self-criticism on a validated scale alongside the interviews, in a sample not recruited to talk about consent. If it replicates there, I'll believe the pattern is in the population and not in the volunteers.

**Nadia:** Agreed. That's the paper — Journal of Sex and Marital Therapy, D-O-I ten point one zero eight zero, slash, zero zero nine two six two three X point two zero two six point two seven four zero six two four. It's open access.

**Theo:** Thanks for listening.

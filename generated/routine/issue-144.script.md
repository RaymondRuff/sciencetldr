## Show notes

**[Recombinant Canine Coronaviruses in Dogs, Europe](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid1601.090726)**

*Emerging Infectious Diseases, 2010*

DOI: [10.3201/eid1601.090726](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid1601.090726)

**Question:** How common in European dogs are canine coronavirus (CCoV) type II strains whose spike 5' end is recombinant with porcine TGEV?

**Answer:** In 1,172 faecal samples from dogs with enteritis in 14 countries (2001-2008), 493 carried CCoV and 78 of 400 CCoV-II-positive samples (19.5%) were TGEV-like subtype IIb — a diagnostic convenience sample with no healthy-dog comparison.

**Three takeaways**

1. About 1 in 5 CCoV-II infections were TGEV-like, but the average is driven by Hungary (31/32); 760 of 1,172 samples came from Italy, where it was 12.3%.
2. Recombination is solid: spike 5' end is 76.6-84.4% identical to TGEV versus 34.2-38.3% to CCoV-IIa, and an ORF7a/7b size assay excluded true TGEV in 20 samples.
3. The suggestion that CCoV-IIa-based vaccines may underperform is a hypothesis; the authors state only vaccination-and-challenge trials could test it.

**Theo:** Okay. So why is that worth ten minutes? A hybrid virus in dogs sounds like veterinary housekeeping. What's the claim?

**Nadia:** Hmm. The honest answer is that it's a counting paper, and the counting had never been done. Before this, the recombinant was a handful of isolates — four strains in Italy, a couple more in the United Kingdom. Nobody knew whether it was a freezer curiosity or established in dogs. The authors say it outright: nobody had determined the prevalence of this subtype.

**Theo:** Right, a different contribution from a mechanism paper. Then set up the virology, because "dog virus carrying pig virus" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

**Nadia:** So. One antigenic group holds the pig virus, transmissible gastroenteritis virus, the cat coronavirus and the dog one. They're so close that the taxonomy committee's own proposal was to call them host-range variants of a single species — above ninety-six percent identical in the main replicase domains.

**Theo:** Mm. So the species boundary is mostly about which animal you found it in.

**Nadia:** Largely. The gene that does differ is the spike, the surface protein that binds the host cell — also the one antibodies mostly see. Dog coronavirus comes in two types, near-identical across the genome but strongly divergent in spike. And in some type two viruses, the first stretch of that gene — the part making the outward-facing tip — is the pig virus version instead. The rest is still a dog virus. That's the subtype they call two-b; the classical ones are two-a.

**Theo:** Okay, I can hold that. So the central claim is that two-b is widespread?

**Nadia:** That it's circulating, in multiple countries, at a share of infections you can't dismiss. They get there with a P-C-R built to exploit that divergence: one primer upstream in a conserved region, then forward into either the classical dog spike or the pig-type one. Whichever reaction amplifies gives you the subtype.

**Theo:** Hold on. That's clean, but the subtype call is then the presence or absence of a band. Those assays fail silently when a primer site drifts — and the premise is that this region is where the variation lives. What happens to a strain whose front end is neither sequence?

**Nadia:** It's invisible, and the paper doesn't test for that. They sequence what amplifies, which confirms the positives, but a negative isn't evidence of absence — and that matters most where they report nothing.

**Theo:** Which is where exactly? Give me the shape of the result.

**Nadia:** One thousand one hundred seventy-two samples. Just over forty-two percent carried any dog coronavirus. Of those, roughly a fifth were type one alone, a bit under half type two alone, and the rest — call it a third — had both at once.

**Theo:** A third carrying both. That's a lot of co-infection.

**Nadia:** It is, though the authors note it's fewer mixed infections than earlier studies reported, not more. Then, within the samples containing type two, about one in five carried the recombinant. Seventy-eight of four hundred.

**Nadia:** It's hiding almost everything. Hungary is thirty-one of thirty-two. The United Kingdom is about a quarter, Italy about an eighth, then single strains from Greece, Romania and Sweden, and nothing from the rest.

**Theo:** And every dog in here already has acute diarrhoea, with no healthy-dog arm anywhere in the paper. So nothing tells me whether carrying the recombinant makes a dog ill.

The one piece of evidence on severity comes from a previous study, not this one — experimental infection of coronavirus-seronegative beagle pups, where the recombinant did what the classical strains do. Mild diarrhoea, a few days, no systemic spread. A new front end, not a new disease.

**Nadia:** Hmm, and one more thing the subtype call isn't. The screening assay quantifies virus, and the loads span orders of magnitude, so a positive covers anything from a trace to a flood. But the subtyping is a band on a gel. Nothing here tells you how much recombinant virus a dog was shedding — which is the quantity you'd want if you were asking whether it spreads well.

**Theo:** Then let's do the recombination claim, because that's the part that could be an artefact. If pig and dog sequence turn up in one reaction, the boring explanation is that these dogs were shedding actual pig virus — they eat things.

**Nadia:** Right, and the control for that is the best thing in the paper. The genomes end differently: dog and cat viruses carry two small accessory genes there, the pig virus one. So one P-C-R across that region gives a short product from pig virus and a much longer one from dog viruses. They ran it on twenty recombinant-positive samples.

**Theo:** And?

**Nadia:** All twenty gave the long product. These are dog viruses carrying a pig-type spike tip, not pig viruses in a dog.

**Theo:** Mm. It's a size readout, not a sequence, so it rules out the one thing it was built to rule out. But it's the right experiment, it's cheap, and plenty of papers would have skipped it. What does the sequencing add?

**Nadia:** They picked twenty-six strains across the regions, sequenced both ends of the spike gene, and got reads from all but two. At the front end these strains sit around eighty percent identical to the pig virus, and only in the mid-thirties to classical dog type two.

**Theo:** Huh. Mid-thirties is barely alignable. That's not drift, that's a different gene in that slot.

**Nadia:** That's the whole argument in one comparison. And then at the back end of the same gene, the same strains are above ninety percent identical to classical dog type two. One virus, two different ancestries, with the join inside a single gene.

**Theo:** Although — careful. At the back end, how close are the pig viruses to the classical dog ones anyway? If they're also high there, that comparison isn't discriminating much, and the front-end flip carries the claim alone.

**Nadia:** They're close. Upper eighties to low nineties for the pig virus, against low to high nineties for the dog one. So the back end only tells you the backbone is dog-like; the front end forces the recombination reading. Which is how the authors frame it too.

**Theo:** So the mechanism is borrowed from other papers and the data is a topology. That's a hypothesis consistent with the tree, not a route. And with sampling this uneven you'd get clustering by country from collection alone.

**Nadia:** That's fair. Though the piece I found genuinely interesting isn't about geography. There's an old pig-type dog strain described about twenty years before this paper — and at the front of spike it sits with the pig viruses, not with these European ones.

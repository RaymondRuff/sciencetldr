## Show notes

**[Global return-on-investment of COVID-19 vaccines in the first year of the vaccination programme: A model-based economic evaluation](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1004772)**

*PLOS Medicine, 2026*

DOI: [10.1371/journal.pmed.1004772](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1004772)

**Question:** Did the $79.4 billion spent developing and delivering COVID-19 vaccines in 2020–21 pay off globally in the first year of use?

**Answer:** Returns per dollar were 59.7 (cost-effectiveness, discounted) to 809 (cost-benefit), all far above 1. The size depends on valuation method and one transmission model's 14–20 million deaths averted.

**Three takeaways**

1. Productivity ($564 billion) plus healthcare ($222 billion) savings alone were about 10 times total costs, with no price on a life.
2. The 809 figure implies benefits of $64.3 trillion, 82.7% of global GDP, beyond the method's marginal-risk range, as the authors note.
3. Everything rests on one counterfactual model; another global estimate put lives saved at 4.1 million.

**Theo:** So everything downstream inherits that model.

**Nadia:** Everything. Then there are two ways to put dollars on those averted deaths and illnesses. The first is a cost-benefit analysis using the value of a statistical life. That's not the worth of a person. It's what people, in surveys and in real trade-offs, are willing to pay for a small cut in their risk of dying, scaled up to one death. The American reference figure is about ten million dollars, and they scale it to each country by income.

**Theo:** Wait — so a death averted in a rich country is worth more than one in a poor country?

**Nadia:** In this method, yes, in proportion to income. Hold onto that. The second method is a cost-effectiveness analysis. Instead of willingness to pay, you count quality-adjusted life years, which are years of life weighted by health, and price each one at roughly what that country's health system would have to spend to buy a healthy year some other way. Those prices are a fraction of income per head, so this method comes out far lower.

**Theo:** And both count the more tangible stuff too?

**Nadia:** Both add averted hospital costs and lost work. And they count lost work conservatively: only the gap until a sick or deceased worker is replaced, not a lifetime of earnings.

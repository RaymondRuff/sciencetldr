## Show notes

**[Recommendations for the use of hormone and nonhormone treatment of vasomotor symptoms of menopause: Menopause Medical Eligibility Criteria (MenoMEC) based on insights from a modified Delphi panel](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002947)**

*Menopause, 2026*

DOI: [10.1097/GME.0000000000002947](https://doi.org/10.1097/GME.0000000000002947)

**Question:** Can a US expert panel agree which hormone and nonhormone treatments for menopausal hot flashes suit women with specific comorbidities?

**Answer:** A 15-physician modified Delphi (11 answered the survey; consensus at ≥70% agreement) built a 1–4 eligibility grid. Online, no comorbidity reached consensus on hormone versus nonhormone therapy; all were settled in person, with no vote tallies reported.

**Three takeaways**

1. Measured agreement covers the uncontroversial: hormone therapy first-line when safe, clonidine unanimously rejected; SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin, oxybutynin and NK-targeted drugs as alternatives.
2. Hormone-or-not for every listed comorbidity missed the 70% bar online; the in-person resolution is expert judgment with no tallies reported.
3. The contraceptive-style grid separates initiation from continuation and relative from absolute risk, so prior VTE is no longer an automatic no.

**Theo:** So it's a guideline, not an experiment. There's no new data in a consensus statement, by design. Why is that worth ten minutes?

**Nadia:** Because the method is the paper. A Delphi is how medicine manufactures agreement when the trials don't exist, and this one lets you look at the machinery. And the format they chose is genuinely clever.

**Nadia:** Quick setup. Vasomotor symptoms, the clinical term for hot flashes and night sweats, are among the most common and disruptive parts of the menopause transition. The standard treatment is hormone therapy: estrogen, plus a progestogen if the woman still has a uterus. But plenty of women can't take it or won't. A past breast cancer, a past blood clot, heart disease. For them, the authors argue, the guidance on alternatives is thin and inconsistent.

**Theo:** And the alternatives are what, exactly?

**Nadia:** Mostly borrowed drugs, used off-label. The S-S-R-I and S-N-R-I antidepressants, gabapentin, which is an anticonvulsant, and oxybutynin, a bladder drug. Then a more recently approved class, the neurokinin-targeted drugs, fezolinetant and elinzanetant.

**Theo:** Okay. Now remind me how a Delphi panel actually works, because I suspect this whole episode hangs on the mechanics.

**Nadia:** Classically, experts rate statements anonymously, see the group's aggregate, and rate again, over several rounds. In the authors' own description, anonymity lets every view carry equal weight, without the pressures of open debate. Theirs is modified: one anonymous online survey, then an in-person meeting where items that hadn't reached agreement were discussed and re-voted. Fifteen physicians, each statement rated on a scale, and an item counted as consensus if seventy percent of panelists landed in the same band, the top third for agreement or the bottom third for disagreement.

**Theo:** Mm. Credit for that before I complain about anything. They wrote the bar down in advance. In episode eighty-seven, the essay on whether the twenty twenty-four election was a mandate, nobody said what result would count, so the verdict couldn't come out the other way. Here the threshold was on paper before anyone voted.

**Nadia:** It is. So here's the central claim as I'd state it: a multidisciplinary American panel reached consensus on which treatments suit which women, and encoded that consensus in a one-to-four eligibility grid. The question is how much of the grid that bar actually covers.

**Theo:** Right. So how many of the fifteen answered the anonymous survey?

**Nadia:** Eleven of the fifteen.

**Theo:** Oh, so the bar was really eight people agreeing, in the one round that's actually anonymous.

**Nadia:** Yes. And, well, the more important part is what failed. **Theo:** Wait. That's every comorbidity. That's the grid.

**Nadia:** It's most of what makes the grid new, yes. In the authors' words, all of those were discussed and resolved during the consensus meeting.

**Nadia:** Hi, and welcome to Science TLDR. I'm Nadia.

**Theo:** And I'm Theo.

**Nadia:** And today we've got a short piece in PS: Political Science and Politics — "Did Donald Trump Receive a Mandate for Sweeping Change in 2024?", by George Edwards. It's part of a symposium on the early second Trump presidency, and the author's answer is no.

**Theo:** Okay. Obvious objection first: the conclusion is in the title, and this is a short single-author essay with no original data collection. Why ten minutes?

**Nadia:** Because the interesting move is the operationalisation. "Mandate" does real institutional work — it gets used to justify acting without Congress — and it's almost never defined tightly enough to be checked. Edwards sets out what would have to be true, then goes to vote returns and public polling. You can audit each step.

**Theo:** Fine. What would have to be true?

**Nadia:** This comes from a long line — Dahl, and his own earlier work. For an election to confer majority support for specific policies, six conditions: voters have opinions on the policies; they know where the candidates stand; the candidates offer the alternatives voters actually want; turnout is large; people vote on issues rather than competence or the economy; and candidate support correlates with policy views.

**Theo:** And the literature says those never all hold.

**Nadia:** Rarely, if ever. His example is 1984 — polling had voters preferring Mondale to Reagan on defence spending, aid to the Contras, environmental protection, civil rights and helping the poor. And most voters still chose Reagan. One vote is a blunt instrument for a bundle of positions.

**Theo:** Mm-hm. So what's the claim being tested?

**Nadia:** He documents that first, which is the right order. Election night: "America has given us an unprecedented and powerful mandate." To Time: "the mandate was massive." The press secretary: a mandate for "transformative, monumental change." And he quotes Trump invoking "one of the largest mandates in presidential history" in a message to Iran about the Houthis — then "an overwhelming mandate" three days later, for impeaching a judge.

**Theo:** That's a lot of jobs for one concept.

**Nadia:** That's his point: it's load-bearing across unrelated domains. So — the vote. Trump won 49.8 percent, just under a majority. Roughly 77 million votes for him; 78.4 million voters did not vote for him. The margin over Harris was about one and a half points.

**Theo:** And in historical terms?

**Nadia:** Fifty presidential elections in the two hundred years before 2024. In forty of them the winner took a larger share of the vote than Trump did. Five had smaller margins — 1844, 1880, 1884, 1960, 1968 — and in five more, including 2016, the winner lost the popular vote outright.

**Theo:** Huh. So bottom fifth.

**Nadia:** He puts it as performing more poorly than eighty percent of predecessors in the era of mass voting. Down-ballot, Republicans lost a House seat and gained four Senate seats, only one from a competitive state — Pennsylvania.

**Theo:** A one-point win is still a win, though. What else?

**Nadia:** Two more strands. First, whether the public itself read it as a mandate. A Fox News poll of registered voters just before inauguration asked exactly that: 42 percent said yes, 51 percent said no. In the same poll, 54 percent called the result mostly a rejection of Biden and Harris; 41 percent called it an endorsement of Trump.

**Theo:** That's the one item where the dependent variable is literally the word in dispute.

**Nadia:** Right. Then delegation. Thirty-two percent of registered voters said he should advance his agenda even if it caused division; 65 percent wanted him to look for ways to work with Democrats. A CBS–YouGov poll: 23 percent said congressional Republicans should back whatever Trump wanted, 77 percent said they should push back when they disagreed. And among Republicans in the public, 55 percent said their own members had no obligation to support policies they disagreed with.

**Theo:** Wait — 55 percent of Republicans?

**Nadia:** That's the Pew figure, yes.

**Theo:** Okay, but I want to flag the next tier. Pew also has 65 percent saying it would be too risky to give Trump more power — and 78 percent opposing giving presidents more power in general.

**Nadia:** Yeah. The second number partly eats the first.

**Theo:** That's my worry. If four in five would refuse any president more power, the Trump-specific item isn't diagnostic. It's measuring the baseline the paper opens with — the anti-government strand in American political culture.

**Nadia:** Fair — he doesn't do that arithmetic. He does report both numbers side by side, so you can.

**Theo:** What about "he campaigned on it"? That's the strongest counter.

**Nadia:** He takes it seriously, and this is the most concrete part. Some things he did campaign on. Others — buying Greenland, Canada becoming part of the United States, retaking the Panama Canal — he reports were never mentioned in any public speech or interview during the campaign. Same for USAID. Same for firing a large share of the doctors and researchers at N-I-H.

**Theo:** And Musk? There the administration's argument is explicit — Vance posted that nobody voted for Elon Musk, but people voted for Trump, who promised to have Musk root out wasteful spending.

**Nadia:** The campaign did promise an efficiency commission with Musk, and he was a surrogate in Pennsylvania. But from an AdImpact analysis: the Trump campaign and outside groups backing him did not mention Musk in any broadcast or cable television ad. One ad by a rapper names him — total spend, six hundred and fifty-eight dollars, aired once.

**Theo:** Oh — six hundred and fifty-eight dollars. That's an actual measurement, not a rhetorical flourish.

**Nadia:** It's the kind of evidence I wanted more of. The top issues in the ads were immigration, inflation, crime and taxes. And a Republican pollster's finding that fewer than half of Trump voters named mass firings or tariffs as a top priority.

**Theo:** Immigration is where I'd expect the mandate case to be strongest.

**Nadia:** It is — conditionally. Two tables make the point better than the prose. A Scripps poll: support for mass deportation of undocumented immigrants, 52 percent. Add "even if it results in fewer people paying into Social Security and Medicare", 48. "Even if the labour market is impacted negatively", 47. "Even if it results in higher prices", 43. "Even if it results in families being separated", 38.

**Theo:** So fourteen points, purely on consequence framing.

**Nadia:** And the second table is sharper. Pew asked which groups should be deported. Violent crimes: 97 percent. Nonviolent crimes: 52. Arrived in the past four years: 44. Those who have a job: 15. Parents of US-born children: 14. People who came as children: 9. Married to a US citizen: 5.

**Theo:** Five.

**Nadia:** Five. And by his fifth week in office, 32 percent of the public said all immigrants here illegally should be deported — 54 percent of Republicans, 10 percent of Democrats. On ending birthright citizenship, one of the clearest campaign promises, support was around a third.

**Theo:** Direction yes, magnitude no.

**Nadia:** That's close to his summary: tightening immigration, not mass deportation; more efficient government, not dismantling agencies. Majorities did back downsizing government in the abstract — and then 52 percent said Trump and Musk had gone too far in cutting federal spending, 57 percent opposed firing tens of thousands of federal workers, 60 percent opposed closing the Department of Education.

**Theo:** Here's my measurement problem. Most of those are post-inauguration. If I'm defending the mandate claim I say: that's a referendum on execution, not on what voters authorised in November.

**Nadia:** Hmm. That's the strongest objection, and he half-anticipates it. His justification is that Trump acted on issues he never campaigned on, so you can't assess authorisation from election-day data alone. I think that works for Greenland or USAID. It's weaker for tariffs and deportation, where pre-election polling exists and he could have leaned on it harder.

**Theo:** And the sourcing? A lot of this is one sentence — "majorities opposed X" — with six parenthetical citations.

**Nadia:** That's my main reservation. His stated strategy is triangulation across many high-quality polls so outliers don't mislead, and everything is public domain. But there's no inclusion rule, no pooling, no uncertainty intervals, and no systematic treatment of question wording — even though Table 1 is itself a demonstration that wording moves support fourteen points.

**Theo:** The thing he shows best is the thing he doesn't control for.

**Nadia:** Yes. And the analysis I actually missed is individual-level: take the 2024 ANES or the Cooperative Election Study and ask whether Trump voters' own policy preferences track the policies, person by person. He cites both datasets — the ANES result comes via another analyst — but never runs that analysis himself.

**Theo:** He also drops the third strand of the mandate literature, right? Perception.

**Nadia:** Explicitly, on space grounds, arguing it matters less here because this president governs unilaterally rather than by moving votes in Congress. Um — reasonable, but unfinished. Perception is the mechanism by which mandates have historically had any effect at all.

**Theo:** This connects to a pattern. Episode 72 — the censorship rhetoric study — randomised the exposure across thirteen thousand respondents, preregistered. Here the design is an audit of found data against a definition.

**Nadia:** Different questions, though. That one asked what rhetoric does to people. This asks whether a claim about an election is true, and you can't randomise an election.

**Theo:** Granted. What I'd borrow is from episode 82, the rabies estimate — whose best feature was stating its own falsification bar numerically. What would count as a mandate? Fifty-five percent of the vote? A majority on the Fox item? He never says, so the verdict can't come out the other way.

**Nadia:** Though he concedes the related point — by these criteria, mandates for transformational change essentially never occur. So the finding isn't that this president failed a test others passed.

**Theo:** Right. Three takeaways.

**Theo:** One: the vote arithmetic isn't in dispute — 49.8 percent, a one-and-a-half-point margin, a House seat lost. Forty of fifty prior elections had a bigger winning share.

**Theo:** Two: the conditional-support tables are the actual finding. Ninety-seven percent for deporting violent criminals, five percent for those married to a citizen, and fourteen points lost once consequences are named.

**Theo:** Three: what I'd believe. That the public didn't endorse these specific policies, and the never-mentioned list — Greenland, USAID, N-I-H — because that's checkable. I wouldn't read post-inauguration approval as evidence about November, and I'd want a stated numerical bar.

**Nadia:** What I'd watch for is someone running that individual-level analysis on the 2024 ANES — vote choice against policy preferences, in the same respondents.

**Theo:** Mine's the threshold, set before an election rather than after. That's the version I'd find persuasive whichever way it came out.

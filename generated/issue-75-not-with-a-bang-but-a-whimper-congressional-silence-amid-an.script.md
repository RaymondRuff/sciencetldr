**Nadia:** Hello, and welcome to Science TLDR. I'm Nadia.

**Theo:** And I'm Theo.

**Nadia:** And today it's Congress and the power of the purse. The paper is "Not with a Bang but a Whimper: Congressional Silence Amid an Historic Assault on the Power of the Purse," by Douglas Kriner at Cornell, in PS: Political Science and Politics, volume 59, pages 456 to 462. The one-line version: Kriner argues the president moved unilaterally on spending and on tariffs, and the branch whose constitutional power that is did almost nothing about it.

**Theo:** And this isn't a data paper, is it?

**Nadia:** No. It's a short analysis article — seven pages — in a section called "Understanding the Early Trump 47 Presidency." Short-form, argument-driven, written close to the events. And I'll flag one thing up front: it's paywalled, so I'm working from the abstract and the literature it sits in. I'll mark clearly what's Kriner's claim versus what's background.

**Theo:** Fair. So why spend ten minutes on an argument piece?

**Nadia:** Because the central claim is harder to evidence than it looks. "Congress offered little resistance" — hmm, resistance measured how, and against what baseline? That's a measurement problem this audience will recognise instantly, whatever they work on.

**Theo:** Start me at the beginning. What is the power of the purse, mechanically?

**Nadia:** Article One, Section Nine: no money shall be drawn from the Treasury but in consequence of appropriations made by law. Congress decides what gets spent. And Article One, Section Eight gives Congress the power to lay duties and imposts — tariffs. Both of those belong to the legislature on paper.

**Theo:** Wait — tariff authority, at least, has been delegated to the president over the last century.

**Nadia:** Right, that's the background condition. It was handed over across a series of statutes, most of which condition it on findings about national security or an emergency. The paper's claim isn't that delegation is new. It's that the scope of the recent exercise is.

**Theo:** And the spending side? What's an impoundment?

**Nadia:** The president declining to spend money Congress appropriated. Nixon did it at scale, and Congress responded with the Impoundment Control Act of nineteen seventy-four, which set a procedure: the president can propose a rescission, Congress has forty-five days of continuous session to agree, and if it doesn't agree, the money has to go out.

**Theo:** Huh. So the statute was written precisely for this situation.

**Nadia:** Written by a Congress that had just been through it. Which is why the abstract's word is "unprecedented impoundments" — the seventy-four Act was supposed to foreclose exactly this.

**Theo:** Okay. Can you break the abstract into its component claims? Because I suspect they're not the same kind of claim.

**Nadia:** Um, so — four. One: the scope and breadth of the unilateral actions is a departure. Two: there were clear statutory violations. Three: there was widespread public opposition. Four: Congress nonetheless offered little resistance, and that's a stark departure from historical precedent. Then a proposed mechanism — partisan incentives, and the president's dominance of his own party.

**Theo:** Those four need completely different evidence.

**Nadia:** They do, and that's the honest thing to say. Claim two is a legal judgment — the kind of thing that rests on institutional authorities like the Government Accountability Office rather than on anything a political scientist measures, though from the abstract I can't tell you which authorities he leans on. Claim three would need survey evidence. Claim four is the one that's genuinely his, and it's the one with the baseline problem.

**Theo:** Unpack the baseline problem.

**Nadia:** To say Congress did less than it historically does, you need a series — something countable across presidencies. Oversight hearings on the disputed accounts. Appropriations riders written to compel obligation. Floor votes on termination resolutions. Subpoenas. Institutional lawsuits. Any of those, coded the same way over time. Without that series, "stark departure" is a narrative comparison to Nixon rather than a measurement.

**Theo:** Hmm. Though a narrative comparison to Nixon isn't nothing. The seventy-four Act is itself the measurement, in a way — that Congress legislated, and this one hasn't.

**Nadia:** That's the strongest version of the argument, and I'd grant it. A Congress that responds to impoundment by rewriting the entire budget process is a high-water mark you can point at.

**Theo:** What about the confound? This is unified government.

**Nadia:** That's what I'd push hardest on. One party holds both chambers and the White House. So "partisan incentives" and "institutional erosion" predict the identical observation, and nothing in this period separates them.

**Theo:** What would separate them?

**Nadia:** The comparison you'd want is a Congress of the president's own party facing an impoundment from a different president of that party. Or the mirror case, an opposition Congress under the same statute. Neither is available here, which is why I read the mechanism as interpretation rather than a tested claim.

**Theo:** There's also the silence problem. Silence is an absence.

**Nadia:** Say more.

**Theo:** Well — Congress bargains privately. A chair makes a call, money moves, nothing enters the public record. And anticipated reaction: if the executive drops a plan because leadership signalled it wouldn't survive, the observable outcome is nothing happening. Which codes as silence.

**Nadia:** Yeah. That cuts both ways and the paper can't adjudicate it from the public record. Though the reverse is also true — a lot of what reads as resistance in the record is a letter that cost nobody anything.

**Theo:** Right, cheap talk inflates the numerator.

**Nadia:** Which is another argument for counting only costly actions. Votes and enacted riders, not correspondence.

**Theo:** And selection? He's picked the cases where Congress stayed quiet.

**Nadia:** That's a real issue with the case-study genre and I'd want it stated out loud. If you sample on the dependent variable you can't estimate how often Congress does push back. In fairness, though, the claim here is about two specific very large episodes, not about a rate.

**Theo:** And the public opinion premise?

**Nadia:** "Widespread public opposition" is load-bearing — it's what makes congressional silence puzzling rather than representative. And polling on tariffs and on spending cuts is extremely sensitive to item wording.

**Theo:** Ah, we've been here. Episode 84, the white nationalism prevalence survey — reverse-keyed wording moved endorsement by nine points on the same construct.

**Nadia:** That's the worry exactly. If public opposition is the premise that makes the silence anomalous, the item wording and the margins have to be on the page.

**Theo:** What's the strongest part of the argument for you?

**Nadia:** The structural piece. The Impoundment Control Act has no self-executing enforcement — the remedy is political, plus whatever the courts decide. If a Congress chooses not to defend its own appropriations, the statute doesn't defend them on its behalf. That's an institutional design observation, not a partisan one, and it holds whichever way the litigation lands.

**Theo:** Which is where this actually gets settled, isn't it. Courts rather than Congress.

**Nadia:** On the live disputes, probably. And I'm deliberately not characterising the litigation status — it's moving, and the article went to press before all of it.

**Theo:** Let me connect this. Episode 72 — the preregistered experiment, just over thirteen thousand US adults, where exposure to real anti-speech quotes from the president and his allies moved his voters toward supporting censorship of the out-party while non-Trump voters backlashed. That's the micro-foundation under "dominance of the party." Legislators aren't afraid of the president as such, they're afraid of that elasticity in their primary electorate.

**Nadia:** And that's evidence this paper doesn't have but could borrow. 72 randomised the exposure; this is a case narrative over the same mechanism. Together they're more persuasive than either alone.

**Theo:** There's a pattern across our observational episodes, actually. The ones that hold up name the number that would falsify them.

**Nadia:** Episode 82, the rabies estimate — three undiagnosed cases in deceased donors scaled to a national figure, which is fragile, but the authors stated that donors would need roughly fourteen-fold elevated rabies risk to break the inference. That's a falsification bar. An analysis piece like this one could do the equivalent: name the number of defections, or of enacted riders, that would have counted as resistance.

**Theo:** Pre-commit to the threshold.

**Nadia:** Mm-hm. Otherwise any level of congressional activity reads as a whimper after the fact.

**Theo:** Give me the credit side before I do takeaways.

**Nadia:** The genre is honest about itself. It's labelled analysis, in a section on an ongoing presidency; it isn't dressed up as a causal study. The abstract situates the episode in broader debates about executive authority and institutional checks, which is the right register for a claim at this evidentiary level. And real-time institutional documentation has value — somebody has to write down what the procedural record looked like while it's still legible.

**Theo:** Okay. Three takeaways. One: the load-bearing claim is comparative — Congress did less than it historically does — and on what's public I don't see the counted series that would support it. Not shown, rather than wrong.

**Nadia:** Agreed.

**Theo:** Two: the mechanism is underdetermined by this period. Partisanship and institutional erosion both predict the same silence under unified government. Three: the structural point is the durable one — the seventy-four Act hands enforcement to a Congress that may have no incentive to enforce it, and that's true regardless of who holds the office.

**Nadia:** I'd add: what I believe on this evidence is that the seventy-four procedures weren't used. What I don't yet believe is the causal story about why.

**Theo:** What would change your mind?

**Nadia:** A counted series. Hearings, riders, termination votes, per Congress, across four or five administrations, coding rules published. If recent Congresses sit well below that distribution, I'd upgrade "departure" from narrative to measurement.

**Theo:** For me it's the next appropriations cycle. If the bills come back with hard obligation language and reporting deadlines attached, then Congress defended itself slowly and quietly and the whimper framing was early. If they don't, Kriner's read looks right.

**Nadia:** And that one resolves in months, not years. That's the episode — PS: Political Science and Politics, volume 59, pages 456 to 462, DOI ten dot one-zero-one-seven slash S-one-zero-four-nine-zero-nine-six-five-two-five-one-zero-one-seven-zero-four. Thanks for listening.
